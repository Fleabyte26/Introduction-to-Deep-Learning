LSTM: a memory with gates

The recurrent network on the previous page remembered an animal for fifteen letters. Its memory is rewritten at every step, so a fact has to survive being overwritten again and again. Make the sentence longer and it fails. The LSTM gives each memory cell three gates: one that decides what to keep, one that decides what to write, and one that decides what to show. A fact can then sit in a cell untouched for as long as it is needed. Both networks below were trained on the same sentences. Make the sentence longer and see which one still knows the last word.

One memory cell, three gates

A gate is a number between 0 and 1 that multiplies something. 0 blocks it completely, 1 lets it all through. This is one cell with the gates in your hands. Try the presets, then the sliders.

Inside the trained LSTM

The real LSTM has 24 cells. One of them, cell 8, turned out to be where it keeps the animal. Nobody chose that cell. Training did. Step through a sentence and watch that one cell for both animals at once: blue is the cat sentence, orange is the dog sentence.

The three gates of that cell at this letter, in the cat sentence

Why it does not fade

On the previous page, a training signal travelling back through the plain network was multiplied at every step and faded to almost nothing. In an LSTM cell, the path back through the memory is multiplied only by the forget gate. If the gate stays near 1, almost nothing is lost. Both lines below are measured from the trained networks.

What it costs

Each gate is a small network of its own, the same size as the whole plain recurrent layer. Three gates plus the new information make four. An LSTM with the same number of cells has four times the weights in its recurrent layer, and does about four times the work at every step. That is the price of a memory that lasts.

From section 10.1 of Dive into Deep Learning. Both networks are real: 21 symbols in, an 8-number embedding, 24 units, trained on 4,000 sentences with 0 to 8 extra words chosen from very, really and quite. The LSTM was trained for 12 passes over the data. The plain network was given 80, nearly seven times as many. The LSTM runs in your browser on this page. The chart counts a sentence as correct when the network’s most likely next letter is the right one, over 200 sentences at each length.
