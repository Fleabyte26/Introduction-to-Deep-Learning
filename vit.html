Vision transformers: a picture as a sentence of patches

A transformer reads a row of vectors. So to give it a picture, cut the picture into small squares, turn each square into a vector, and line them up like words. That is the whole idea. This page runs a small real vision transformer in your browser, shows what it has to learn that a convolutional network gets for free, and measures what that costs when training pictures are scarce.

It has to learn where the patches are

A convolutional network is built knowing that pixels next to each other belong together. A vision transformer is not. Each position starts with a random tag of 32 numbers, and training has to work out the layout of the picture from scratch. Click a square to see which other positions ended up with a similar tag.

What this page left out

Scale. The famous result is that with enough pictures, hundreds of millions, vision transformers overtake convolutional networks. This page cannot show that. Its largest experiment has 60,000 small grey pictures, and the convolutional network is still ahead there. What it shows is the direction: the gap closing as the data grows.

Middle paths. Later designs give some of the picture knowledge back. The Swin transformer, covered at the end of section 11.8, only lets nearby patches attend to each other at first and merges patches as it goes deeper, much like pooling.

The network in the first panel is real: 19,402 weights, two blocks, two heads, patches of 4 by 4 pixels, trained on the 60,000 Fashion-MNIST training pictures and right on 85.1% of the 10,000 test pictures. Every number on this page is a real training run, two random starts for each point. The training code is in the repository under experiments. Goes with section 11.8 of Dive into Deep Learning.
