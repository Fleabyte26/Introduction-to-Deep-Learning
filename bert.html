Pretraining and BERT: learn by filling in the blank

Labelled examples are expensive. Plain text is free. BERT is a way to turn free text into a trained network: hide a word, make the network guess it from the words on both sides, and repeat a few billion times. Nobody labels anything, because the text is its own answer key. This page runs two small real networks trained that way, one that reads in both directions and one that cannot, and measures what the trained network is worth when labels are scarce.

The training recipe

Take any text. Pick 15 words in every 100 at random. Most of them are replaced by a blank. A few are swapped for a random word, and a few are left alone, so the network cannot assume that a word it can see is the right one. Then it has to say what every picked word really was.

No person labelled anything here. The right answers are the words that were covered up. That is why this can be done on billions of words: the only cost is computing.

What the pretrained network is worth

A new job: decide whether a sentence makes sense. "The cat sat on the mat" does. "The cat sat on the hive" does not. Suppose only a few sentences come with the right answer attached. Three ways to do it, each tried for real.

That is BERT-base, the smaller of the two models in the 2018 paper: about 110 million weights. The 12 blocks are the same block counted in the transformer episode, with vectors of 768 numbers. One detail matters for the experiment above: BERT was pretrained on two tasks. Filling in blanks, and judging whether one sentence follows another. The second task is what trains the [CLS] slot.

What this page left out

Scale, again. The two networks here have 11,801 weights and learned from 336 sentences about twelve animals. BERT learned from about 3.3 billion words. The mechanism is the same. What it can know is not.
