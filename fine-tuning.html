Fine-tuning: borrow a network, replace its last layer

You want a network that recognises hot dogs, and you have 2,000 photos. ImageNet has more than ten million. A network trained from nothing on 2,000 photos will mostly memorise them. But a network that has already seen ten million photos knows what edges, textures and shapes look like, and none of that is specific to ImageNet. Fine-tuning keeps all of that knowledge and replaces only the part that names the answer. It takes four steps. Press Play.

Why two learning rates

After step 3 the model is in two very different states at once. The copied layers are already close to good: they need a nudge. The new output layer is random: it needs to learn everything. One learning rate cannot suit both. Below is a cartoon of that problem with just two numbers, one for the copied layers and one for the new layer. The book’s experiment, and one detail that bites

Hot dogs. 1,400 photos with a hot dog and 1,400 photos of other food. 1,000 of each for training, the rest for testing. The source model is ResNet-18 pretrained on ImageNet. It is trained for 5 epochs with a learning rate of 0.00005 for the copied layers and ten times that for the new output layer. The same network trained from random weights, with a ten times larger learning rate everywhere, does worse over the same 5 epochs. The book’s explanation is short: the fine-tuned model starts from better values.

The detail that bites. The copied layers expect pictures prepared exactly the way ImageNet pictures were prepared when the source model was trained: the same size, and each colour channel shifted and scaled by the same three averages and three spreads. Feed them anything else and the borrowed knowledge does not line up with what it sees. This is why every fine-tuning script has those six odd-looking numbers at the top.

Where augmentation comes back. The training pictures get a random crop resized to 224 by 224 and a random left-right flip. The test pictures get no randomness: resized to 256, then the centre 224 by 224 cut out. That is the rule from the image augmentation page: random for training, fixed for testing.
