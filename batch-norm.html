Batch normalization: resetting the scale at every layer

Deep in a network, the numbers arriving at a layer can drift far from zero or spread very wide, and they keep moving as the earlier layers train. Batch normalization takes each minibatch, subtracts its average, divides by its spread, and then lets the network choose a new scale and shift. During training, an example's output depends on which other examples happened to land in its minibatch, because they set the average and the spread. That wobble is noise, and like dropout it acts as a mild regularizer. Small minibatches wobble a lot, large ones hardly at all.

What to take from this

The formula is BN(x) = γ × (x − μ) / σ + β, where μ and σ are the average and spread of the current minibatch, computed separately for every unit (or for every channel in a convolutional layer). After the subtraction and division, the values always have average 0 and spread 1, whatever arrived. γ and β are ordinary learned parameters, so the network is free to pick whatever scale and position works best. The difference is that it now picks them directly, in two numbers, and they no longer shift every time an earlier layer changes.

At prediction time there may be only one example, so there is no minibatch to measure. The layer uses running averages of μ and σ that it collected during training. That is why a batch normalization layer behaves differently in training mode and prediction mode, the same way dropout does.

From Section 8.5 of Dive into Deep Learning. The book places batch normalization after the linear or convolutional step and before the activation function, and notes that a minibatch of size 1 teaches nothing: after subtracting its own average, every value is zero. Batch normalization lets deep networks train with larger learning rates and converge faster. Why it works so well is still argued about. The original explanation, called internal covariate shift, is disputed, and the book says so plainly. The flat zone shown here is where the sigmoid's slope is close to zero, the vanishing-gradient problem from the sigmoid page.
