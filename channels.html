Where the channels go

A colour image has three channels. The first layer of a real network turns them into sixty-four. Nothing in the picture got deeper, so where do sixty-four come from, and what happened to the three? This is the part of Chapter 7 that diagrams usually get wrong. Set the two counts and watch one output channel get built.

The two rules

Input channels are summed away. The kernel is not a square, it is a stack: one k×k slice for every input channel. Each slice slides over its own channel, and the results are added together, pixel by pixel. However many channels go in, one number comes out per position. That is why the picture above has several rows on the left and a single plane on the right.

Output channels are a choice. To get a second output channel you need a second complete stack of slices, with its own weights. Sixty-four output channels means sixty-four stacks. The input count is decided for you by the previous layer; the output count is a number you pick, and it is the main dial for how big the layer is.

What a channel means. At the input, channels are red, green and blue. After the first layer they are no longer colours; each one is a map of where some pattern was found. One may respond to vertical edges, another to a particular texture. The network decides what, by training.

Why this is where the parameters live

Parameters in a convolution do not depend on the size of the image at all. A 3×3 kernel over a 224×224 image has exactly as many weights as over a 28×28 one. They depend on the two channel counts, multiplied together:

LayerKernelWeights+ biases
First layer of VGG: RGB to 643×3, 3 in, 64 out1,7281,792
A deep VGG layer: 512 to 5123×3, 512 in, 512 out2,359,2962,359,808
A 1×1 bottleneck: 64 to 2561×1, 64 in, 256 out16,38416,640

The middle row is the whole story of why deep networks got expensive. Doubling the channel count quadruples the weights in that layer, because both counts appear in the product. It is also why the 1×1 convolution in the third row matters so much: it changes the channel count cheaply, with no spatial mixing at all, which is exactly what ResNet uses it for.
