The gap says how much of the model's skill does not survive contact with new data. Test accuracy says how good it actually is. The two are different questions, and a technique can win one and lose the other.

  


  
What to take from this

  
All five models have the same shape: 784 inputs, three hidden layers of 256, 128 and 64 units, and 10 outputs. Nothing here changes the data or the size of the network. What changes is one argument, one extra layer, or one callback, and the four techniques attach at three different points in the same five lines: inside a layer, between layers, or on the call to fit.

  
Dropout wins on both counts: it closes the gap to half a point and has the best test accuracy. Early stopping and L2 each leave about three points of gap and land in the middle, by different routes, one by halting after 11 epochs and the other by holding the weights down for all 30. Batch normalization does the opposite of what the word "regularization" suggests: it stabilises training so well that the model fits the training set harder, ending with the widest gap of all five and the worst test score. It is a training aid, not a restraint.

  
The band from 0.862 to 0.888 is narrow. None of these techniques made the network substantially better at recognising clothing; they decided whether its training numbers could be believed. Getting past roughly 0.90 on Fashion-MNIST takes a different architecture, not a better regulariser.


  
Curves and scores are from a single clean run of the whole notebook: 60,000 training rows split 60/20/20 into 36,000 train, 12,000 validation and 12,000 test; Adam; batch size 128; 30 epochs, except early stopping, which halted itself after 11.
